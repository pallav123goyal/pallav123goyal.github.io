Lecture 14: Surface Integrals

Recall: Given a parametrized surface \(S\), its surface area can be computed using the double integral: \[\iint_{S} \|T_u \times T_v\|\,du\,dv\]

Surface Integrals of Scalar Functions

Definition: Given a scalar-valued function \(f(x, y, z)\), its integral over a parametrized surface \(S\) is defined as: \[\iint_{S} f(r(u, v)) \|T_u \times T_v\|\,du\,dv\]

Examples

Example 1

Integrate the function \(f(x, y, z) = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + 1}\) over the surface parametrized by \(r(r, \theta) = \langle r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta, \theta \rangle\) for \(0 \le r \le 1\) and \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\).

Solution:

First, we find the tangent vectors by taking the partial derivatives of the parametrization with respect to \(r\) and \(\theta\):

\[T_r = \langle \cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0 \rangle\] \[T_\theta = \langle -r\sin\theta, r\cos\theta, 1 \rangle\]

Next, we compute the cross product \(T_r \times T_\theta\):

\[T_r \times T_\theta = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ \cos\theta & \sin\theta & 0 \\ -r\sin\theta & r\cos\theta & 1 \end{vmatrix} = \sin\theta\hat{i} - \cos\theta\hat{j} + r\hat{k}\]

We then find the magnitude of this cross product vector:

\[\|T_r \times T_\theta\| = \sqrt{\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta + r^2} = \sqrt{1 + r^2}\]

Now, we substitute the surface components \(x = r\cos\theta\) and \(y = r\sin\theta\) into the scalar function \(f(x, y, z)\):

\[f(x, y, z) = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + 1} = \sqrt{r^2\cos^2\theta + r^2\sin^2\theta + 1} = \sqrt{r^2 + 1}\]

Finally, we set up and evaluate the surface integral over the given region parameters:

\[\int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{0}^{1} \left(\sqrt{1 + r^2}\right)\left(\sqrt{r^2 + 1}\right) \,dr\,d\theta = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{0}^{1} (1 + r^2) \,dr\,d\theta\] \[= \int_{0}^{2\pi} \left[ r + \frac{r^3}{3} \right]_{0}^{1} d\theta = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \frac{4}{3} \,d\theta = \frac{8\pi}{3}\]
Example 2

Compute \(\iint_{S} x\,dS\) over the surface which is the graph of the function \(z = f(x, y) = x^2 + y\) given the bounds \(0 \le x \le 1\) and \(-1 \le y \le 1\).

Solution:

We first establish the standard parametrization for a function graph over \(x\) and \(y\):

\[x = x, \quad y = y, \quad z = x^2 + y\]

with the bounds \(0 \le x \le 1\) and \(-1 \le y \le 1\).

Compute the tangent vectors:

\[T_x = \langle 1, 0, 2x \rangle\] \[T_y = \langle 0, 1, 1 \rangle\]

Compute their cross product vector:

\[T_x \times T_y = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 1 & 0 & 2x \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = -2x\hat{i} - \hat{j} + \hat{k}\]

Find the magnitude element \(\|T_x \times T_y\|\):

\[\|T_x \times T_y\| = \sqrt{(-2x)^2 + (-1)^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{4x^2 + 2}\]

The resulting surface integral setup becomes:

\[\int_{-1}^{1} \int_{0}^{1} x\sqrt{4x^2 + 2} \,dx\,dy\]

Since the integrand does not depend on \(y\), integrating over \(dy\) from \(-1\) to \(1\) simply multiplies the inner integral by a factor of 2:

\[= \int_{0}^{1} 2x\sqrt{4x^2 + 2} \,dx\]

We solve this using \(u\)-substitution, setting \(u = 4x^2 + 2\), which gives \(du = 8x\,dx\), or \((1/4)du = 2x\,dx\):

\[\frac{1}{4} \int \sqrt{u} \,du = \frac{1}{4} \left[ \frac{2}{3}u^{3/2} \right] = \frac{1}{6} \left[ (4x^2 + 2)^{3/2} \right]_{0}^{1}\] \[= \frac{1}{6} (6^{3/2} - 2^{3/2})\]
Example 3

Compute \(\iint_{S} y\,dS\) where \(S\) is the triangular region with vertices \((1,0,0)\), \((0,2,0)\), and \((0,0,3)\).

Visual Description of the Surface: A 3D Cartesian coordinate diagram showing a shaded triangular plane surface embedded in the first octant. The triangle acts as an inclined flat face intersecting the positive coordinate axes at three intercept points: \((1,0,0)\) on the x-axis, \((0,2,0)\) on the y-axis, and \((0,0,3)\) on the z-axis.

Solution:

The standard intercept equation of the plane passing through these three points is given by:

\[\frac{x}{1} + \frac{y}{2} + \frac{z}{3} = 1\]

We choose to parametrize this surface explicitly by solving for \(x\) in terms of \(y\) and \(z\):

\[x = 1 - \frac{y}{2} - \frac{z}{3}, \quad y = y, \quad z = z\]

Find the corresponding tangent vectors over the parameter space:

\[T_y = \langle -\frac{1}{2}, 1, 0 \rangle\] \[T_z = \langle -\frac{1}{3}, 0, 1 \rangle\]

Compute the normal vector via cross product:

\[T_y \times T_z = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ -\frac{1}{2} & 1 & 0 \\ -\frac{1}{3} & 0 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = \hat{i} + \frac{1}{2}\hat{j} + \frac{1}{3}\hat{k}\]

Calculate its magnitude:

\[\|T_y \times T_z\| = \sqrt{1^2 + \left(\frac{1}{4}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{9}\right)} = \sqrt{\frac{36 + 9 + 4}{36}} = \sqrt{\frac{49}{36}} = \frac{7}{6}\]

Thus, the surface integral transforms into a double integral over the projection on the \(yz\)-plane:

\[\iint_{D} y \cdot \frac{7}{6} \,dy\,dz\]
Visual Description of the Proportional Projection: A 2D diagram tracking the region of integration on the \(yz\)-plane. The domain is bounded by the positive y-axis, the positive z-axis, and a downward-sloping linear line connecting the intercept points \((0,3)\) on the vertical z-axis and \((2,0)\) on the horizontal y-axis. The region forms a right triangle.

To establish the integration boundaries from the boundary line equation \(\frac{y}{2} + \frac{z}{3} = 1\), we express \(y\) as a function of \(z\):

\[y = 2\left(1 - \frac{z}{3}\right) = 2 - \frac{2z}{3}\]

Hence, the integration limits are \(0 \le z \le 3\) and \(0 \le y \le 2 - \frac{2z}{3}\).

Setting up and evaluating the double integral:

\[\frac{7}{6} \int_{0}^{3} \int_{0}^{2 - \frac{2z}{3}} y \,dy\,dz = \frac{7}{6} \int_{0}^{3} \left[ \frac{y^2}{2} \right]_{0}^{2 - \frac{2z}{3}} \,dz\] \[= \frac{7}{6} \int_{0}^{3} \frac{1}{2} \left(2 - \frac{2z}{3}\right)^2 \,dz\]

Factoring out a 2 from inside the squared term gives \((2(1 - z/3))^2 = 4(1 - z/3)^2\):

\[= \frac{7}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot 4 \int_{0}^{3} \left(1 - \frac{z}{3}\right)^2 \,dz = \frac{7}{3} \int_{0}^{3} \left(1 - \frac{z}{3}\right)^2 \,dz\]

Using standard integration rules:

\[= \frac{7}{3} \left[ \frac{(1 - \frac{z}{3})^3}{3 \cdot (-\frac{1}{3})} \right]_{0}^{3} = \frac{7}{3} \left[ \frac{(1 - \frac{z}{3})^3}{-1} \right]_{0}^{3}\] \[= \frac{7}{3} \left[ 0 - (-1) \right] = \frac{7}{3}\]

Surface Integrals of Vector Fields

Definition: A vector field (or a vector-valued function) in three dimensions is a mapping \(F: \mathbb{R}^3 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^3\). Given a vector field \(F\) and a smooth parametrized surface \(S\) defined by \(r(u,v)\), the surface integral of \(F\) over \(S\) is defined as: \[\iint_{S} F \cdot dS = \iint_{D} F(r(u, v)) \cdot (T_u \times T_v) \,du\,dv\]
Example 4

Let \(F = 2x\hat{i} + 2y\hat{j} + z^2\hat{k}\) and let the surface \(S\) be parametrized by \(r(u, v) = \langle 2\sin u, 3\cos u, v \rangle\) with parameter bounds \(0 \le u \le 2\pi\) and \(0 \le v \le 1\). Compute \(\iint_{S} F \cdot dS\).

Solution:

First, calculate the partial tangent vectors along the parameters \(u\) and \(v\):

\[T_u = \langle 2\cos u, -3\sin u, 0 \rangle\] \[T_v = \langle 0, 0, 1 \rangle\]

Compute the cross product vector component:

\[T_u \times T_v = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 2\cos u & -3\sin u & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = -3\sin u\hat{i} - 2\cos u\hat{j} + 0\hat{k}\]

Substitute the surface tracking coordinates \(x = 2\sin u\), \(y = 3\cos u\), and \(z = v\) into the original field expression \(F\):

\[F(r(u,v)) = 2(2\sin u)\hat{i} + 2(3\cos u)\hat{j} + v^2\hat{k} = 4\sin u\hat{i} + 6\cos u\hat{j} + v^2\hat{k}\]

Now, compute the dot product inside the integral expression: \(F \cdot (T_u \times T_v)\):

\[F \cdot (T_u \times T_v) = (4\sin u)(-3\sin u) + (6\cos u)(-2\cos u) + (v^2)(0)\] \[= -12\sin^2 u - 12\cos^2 u = -12(\sin^2 u + \cos^2 u) = -12\]

Set up and evaluate the final double definite integral across boundaries:

\[\int_{0}^{1} \int_{0}^{2\pi} -12 \,du\,dv = \int_{0}^{1} \left[ -12u \right]_{0}^{2\pi} \,dv = \int_{0}^{1} -24\pi \,dv = -24\pi\]
Physical Interpretation: The vector surface integral \(\iint_{S} F \cdot dS\) computes the net volume flow rate, or flux, of the vector field through the designated surface sheet \(S\).
Remark on Orientation: In the previous vector problem, if we had calculated the cross product ordering as \(T_v \times T_u\) instead of \(T_u \times T_v\), the normal vector orientation flips direction (\(T_v \times T_u = - (T_u \times T_v)\)). This changes the sign of the output result to \(+24\pi\). Unless an explicit boundary orientation is specified by the problem, either cross product configuration can be structurally evaluated to find the surface integral.
