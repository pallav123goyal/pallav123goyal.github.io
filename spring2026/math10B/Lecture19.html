Lecture 19: Gauss' / Divergence Theorem

Theorem: Gauss' / Divergence Theorem

Given a vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) and a solid region \(R\) enclosed by a boundary surface \(\partial R\) oriented with an outward unit normal vector, the theorem states:

\[\iiint_{R} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} \, dV = \iint_{\partial R} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}\]

In conceptual terms, this means that the integral of the divergence of \(\mathbf{F}\) over the region \(R\) is exactly equal to the net integral flux of \(\mathbf{F}\) across its bounding surface.

Worked Examples

Example 1

Consider the vector field \(\mathbf{F} = 2x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + y^2\hat{\mathbf{j}} + z^2\hat{\mathbf{k}}\). Compute the surface integral of \(\mathbf{F}\) over the unit sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\).

Solution:

First, let's explore how we would set this up if we attempted to compute this surface integral directly. We begin by parametrizing the unit sphere surface using spherical coordinates:

\[x = \sin\phi \cos\theta\] \[y = \sin\phi \sin\theta\] \[z = \cos\phi\]

where the domain parameters range over \(0 \le \phi \le \pi\) and \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\).

The standard tangent vectors with respect to our parameters \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) are:

\[\mathbf{T}_\theta = \langle -\sin\phi \sin\theta, \sin\phi \cos\theta, 0 \rangle\] \[\mathbf{T}_\phi = \langle \cos\phi \cos\theta, \cos\phi \sin\theta, -\sin\phi \rangle\]

Next, we compute the standard cross product normal vector element \(\mathbf{T}_\theta \times \mathbf{T}_\phi\):

\[\mathbf{T}_\theta \times \mathbf{T}_\phi = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{i}} & \hat{\mathbf{j}} & \hat{\mathbf{k}} \\ -\sin\phi\sin\theta & \sin\phi\cos\theta & 0 \\ \cos\phi\cos\theta & \cos\phi\sin\theta & -\sin\phi \end{vmatrix}\] \[= -\sin^2\phi \cos\theta \hat{\mathbf{i}} - \sin^2\phi \sin\theta \hat{\mathbf{j}} - \sin\phi \cos\phi \hat{\mathbf{k}}\]

Since we require an outward normal direction, our properly oriented normal surface element points in the positive radial direction:

\[\text{Outward normal element} = \sin^2\phi \cos\theta \hat{\mathbf{i}} + \sin^2\phi \sin\theta \hat{\mathbf{j}} + \sin\phi \cos\phi \hat{\mathbf{k}}\]

Finally, we would substitute the parametrization into the field \(\mathbf{F}\), compute the dot product \(\mathbf{F} \cdot (\mathbf{T}_\theta \times \mathbf{T}_\phi)\), and carry out the long double integral. This directly is quite computationally intensive.

Alternative approach using Gauss' Theorem:

Instead of the complex surface mapping, we can seamlessly evaluate this integral over the interior solid region of the unit sphere \(R\) bounded by \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\) using the divergence theorem:

\[\iint_{\partial R} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint_{R} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} \, dV\]

First, we compute the divergence of our field \(\mathbf{F} = 2x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + y^2\hat{\mathbf{j}} + z^2\hat{\mathbf{k}}\):

\[\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(2x) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(y^2) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(z^2) = 2 + 2y + 2z\]

We switch to spherical coordinate bounds to parametrize the solid ball region \(R\):

\[x = r\sin\phi\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\phi\sin\theta, \quad z = r\cos\phi\]

where \(0 \le r \le 1\), \(0 \le \phi \le \pi\), and \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\). Recall that the volume differential element is given by \(dV = r^2 \sin\phi \, dr \, d\theta \, d\phi\).

Setting up our volume integral yields:

\[\int_{0}^{\pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{0}^{1} (2 + 2r\sin\phi\sin\theta + 2r\cos\phi) r^2 \sin\phi \, dr \, d\theta \, d\phi\]

We can separate this linear expression into three distinct integral components to compute independently:

  1. First term: \(\int_{0}^{\pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{0}^{1} 2r^2 \sin\phi \, dr \, d\theta \, d\phi = \left( \int_{0}^{1} 2r^2 \, dr \right) \left( \int_{0}^{2\pi} d\theta \right) \left( \int_{0}^{\pi} \sin\phi \, d\phi \right) = \left(\frac{2}{3}\right) (2\pi) (2) = \frac{8\pi}{3}\)
  2. Second term: \(\int_{0}^{\pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{0}^{1} 2r^3 \sin^2\phi \sin\theta \, dr \, d\theta \, d\phi = 0\), due to the fact that \(\int_{0}^{2\pi} \sin\theta \, d\theta = 0\).
  3. Third term: \(\int_{0}^{\pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{0}^{1} 2r^3 \sin\phi \cos\phi \, dr \, d\theta \, d\phi = 0\), because \(\int_{0}^{\pi} \sin\phi \cos\phi \, d\phi = \left[ \frac{1}{2}\sin^2\phi \right]_0^\pi = 0\).

Summing up our three completed integral components yields:

\[\text{Total Integral} = \frac{8\pi}{3} + 0 + 0 = \frac{8\pi}{3}\]
Example 2

Given the vector field \(\mathbf{F} = xy^2\hat{\mathbf{i}} + x^2y\hat{\mathbf{j}} + y\hat{\mathbf{k}}\). Compute the surface flux integral over the boundary of the cylinder defined by \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\) and bounded vertically by the planes \(z = 1\) and \(z = -1\).

Visual Description of the Bounding Surface:

The figure illustrates a three-dimensional vertical cylinder centered along the vertical z-axis. The total bounding closed surface is composed of three interconnected smooth pieces: a top flat circular boundary cap labeled \(S_1\) located on the plane \(z = 1\), a vertical curved outer wall labeled \(S_2\) satisfying the lateral surface constraint \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\), and a bottom flat circular boundary cap labeled \(S_3\) located on the plane \(z = -1\). The cartesian coordinate axes \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\) project out normally from the origin to establish the orientation framework.

Solution:

To evaluate this over each of the three pieces directly, one would have to parameterize via cylindrical coordinates:

  • On the top disc surface \(S_1\), \(z = 1\) with \(r \le 1\).
  • On the lateral wall surface \(S_2\), \(r = 1\) with \(-1 \le z \le 1\).
  • On the bottom disc surface \(S_3\), \(z = -1\) with \(r \le 1\).

For each separate surface, one needs to derive cross-products to find normal orientation vectors, compute individual surface integrals, and finally add them together. Alternatively, we can use Gauss' Theorem over the inner cylindrical volume \(R\).

By applying Gauss' Theorem, the net surface flux is equivalent to the volume integral of the divergence over the solid interior:

\[\iiint_{R} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} \, dV\]

Let's compute the divergence of our field \(\mathbf{F} = xy^2\hat{\mathbf{i}} + x^2y\hat{\mathbf{j}} + y\hat{\mathbf{k}}\):

\[\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(xy^2) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^2y) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(y) = y^2 + x^2\]

In cylindrical coordinates, we note that \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\). The boundary conditions defining our solid cylindrical region \(R\) translate elegantly to:

\[0 \le r \le 1, \quad 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi, \quad -1 \le z \le 1\]

Accounting for the cylindrical volume expansion element Jacobian \(dV = r \, dr \, d\theta \, dz\), the integral becomes:

\[\int_{-1}^{1} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{0}^{1} (r^2) \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta \, dz = \int_{-1}^{1} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{0}^{1} r^3 \, dr \, d\theta \, dz\]

Evaluating the decoupled pieces step-by-step:

\[\int_{0}^{1} r^3 \, dr = \left[ \frac{r^4}{4} \right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{4}\] \[\int_{0}^{2\pi} \frac{1}{4} \, d\theta = \frac{2\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{2}\] \[\int_{-1}^{1} \frac{\pi}{2} \, dz = \frac{\pi}{2} \cdot \big(1 - (-1)\big) = \pi\]

Thus, the final integrated value is \(\pi\).

Example 3

Suppose \(\mathbf{F} = x^3\hat{\mathbf{i}} + y^3z^2\hat{\mathbf{j}} + xyz\hat{\mathbf{k}}\). Integrate the vector field curl, \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F}\), over the closed surface of the sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4\).

Solution Method I: Using Gauss' Theorem

According to Gauss' theorem, the surface integral of a field over a closed boundary matches the volume integral of its divergence throughout the interior region \(R\):

\[\iint_{\partial R} (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint_{R} \nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \, dV\]

Let's first determine the curl field \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F}\) explicitly by setting up its determinant format:

\[\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{i}} & \hat{\mathbf{j}} & \hat{\mathbf{k}} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ x^3 & y^3z^2 & xyz \end{vmatrix}\] \[= \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(xyz) - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(y^3z^2) \right)\hat{\mathbf{i}} - \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(xyz) - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(x^3) \right)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(y^3z^2) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^3) \right)\hat{\mathbf{k}}\] \[= (xz - 2y^3z)\hat{\mathbf{i}} - yz\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 0\hat{\mathbf{k}}\]

Now, we compute the divergence of this curl vector field:

\[\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(xz - 2y^3z) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(-yz) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(0)\] \[= z - z + 0 = 0\]

Since the integrand is zero everywhere, the entire triple volume integral naturally vanishes to zero.

Solution Method II: Using Stokes' Theorem

By Stokes' Theorem, we can relate the surface integral of a curl to a line integral traversing the boundary curve \(C\) of our surface layer:

\[\iint_{S} (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \oint_{C} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\]

However, a complete spherical shell is a closed surface, meaning it possesses no boundary curve (\(C = \emptyset\)). Consequently, the line integral along the non-existent boundary path is zero, confirming our previous result.

General Differential and Integral Vector Properties

Property 1: Divergence of a Curl

For any twice continuously differentiable vector field \(\mathbf{F} = F_1\hat{\mathbf{i}} + F_2\hat{\mathbf{j}} + F_3\hat{\mathbf{k}}\), the divergence of its curl is identically zero:

\[\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) = 0\]

This is easily verified by expanding the partial derivatives:

\[\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \left(\frac{\partial F_3}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial z}\right)\hat{\mathbf{i}} + \left(\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial F_3}{\partial x}\right)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + \left(\frac{\partial F_2}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial y}\right)\hat{\mathbf{k}}\]

Taking the divergence yields a sum of mixed second-order partial derivatives that cancel out by Clairaut's Theorem: \(\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) = 0\).

Property 2: Divergence-Free Vector Fields

Conversely, if a vector field \(\mathbf{G}\) satisfies \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{G} = 0\) on a simply connected domain, then \(\mathbf{G}\) must be the curl of some vector field \(\mathbf{F}\):

\[\nabla \cdot \mathbf{G} = 0 \iff \mathbf{G} = \nabla \times \mathbf{F} \quad \text{for some field } \mathbf{F}\]

Such a vector field \(\mathbf{G}\) is formally referred to as a divergence-free or solenoidal vector field.

Property 3: Curl of a Gradient

For any scalar function \(f\), the curl of its gradient is identically zero:

\[\nabla \times (\nabla f) = \mathbf{0}\]

We see this immediately by representing the gradient field component layout:

\[\nabla f = \left\langle \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} \right\rangle\]

Computing the cross product determinant produces zero terms throughout due to the equality of cross-partial derivatives.

Property 4: Curl-Free Vector Fields

Conversely, if a vector field \(\mathbf{G}\) satisfies \(\nabla \times \mathbf{G} = \mathbf{0}\) on a simply connected region, then \(\mathbf{G}\) must be the gradient of some scalar function \(f\):

\[\nabla \times \mathbf{G} = \mathbf{0} \iff \mathbf{G} = \nabla f \quad \text{for some scalar function } f\]

Recall that such a vector field \(\mathbf{G}\) is called a conservative or curl-free (irrotational) vector field.
