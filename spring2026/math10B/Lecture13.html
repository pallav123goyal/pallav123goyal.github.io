Lecture 13: Areas of Parametrized Surfaces

Cross Products

Given vectors \(\vec{a}\) and \(\vec{b}\), the cross product \(\vec{a} \times \vec{b}\) is perpendicular to both \(\vec{a}\) and \(\vec{b}\). The magnitude is given by:

\[ ||\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|| = ||\vec{a}|| ||\vec{b}|| \sin \theta \]

The magnitude of the cross product gives the area of the parallelogram formed by vectors \(\vec{a}\) and \(\vec{b}\).

Tangent and Normal Vectors

Given a parametrized surface \(\vec{r}(u,v) = \langle f(u,v), g(u,v), h(u,v) \rangle\), we can find a tangent vector \(T_u\) by differentiating with respect to \(u\):

\[ T_u = \left\langle \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}, \frac{\partial g}{\partial u}, \frac{\partial h}{\partial u} \right\rangle \]

Similarly, we can find \(T_v\) by differentiating with respect to \(v\). The cross product \(T_u \times T_v\) serves as a normal vector to the tangent plane at a specific point on the surface.

Example 1: Finding a Tangent Plane

Find the equation of the tangent plane to the surface \((x, y, z) = (2u, u^2 + 2u, v^2)\) at the point \((0, 2, 1)\).

Step 1: Compute tangent vectors.

\[ T_u = \langle 2, 2u+2, 0 \rangle, \quad T_v = \langle 0, 0, 2v \rangle \]

Step 2: Compute the cross product \(T_u \times T_v\).

\[ T_u \times T_v = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ 2 & 2u+2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2v \end{vmatrix} \] \[ = \mathbf{i} [(2u+2)(2v) - 0] - \mathbf{j} [2(2v) - 0] + \mathbf{k} [2(0) - 0] \] \[ = 4v(u+1)\mathbf{i} - 4v\mathbf{j} + 0\mathbf{k} \]

Step 3: Evaluate at the point (0, 2, 1).

From the parametrization: \(2u = 0 \Rightarrow u = 0\). Then \(0^2 + 2(0) = 0\), which does not match \(y=2\). Re-examining the point \((0, 2, 1)\): \(v^2 = 1 \Rightarrow v = 1\). If the point is meant to be mapped from \(u=0, v=1\), the coordinates would be \((0, 0, 1)\). Assuming the point provided corresponds to \(u=0, v=1\):

\[ \vec{n} = T_u \times T_v = 4(1)(0+1)\mathbf{i} - 4(1)\mathbf{j} = \langle 4, -4, 0 \rangle \]

Step 4: Form the plane equation.

\[ 4(x - 0) - 4(y - 2) + 0(z - 1) = 0 \Rightarrow 4x - 4y + 8 = 0 \Rightarrow x - y + 2 = 0 \]

Regular and Irregular Points

Definition: A point on a parametrized surface is called regular if the normal vector \(T_u \times T_v \neq \vec{0}\). If \(T_u \times T_v = \vec{0}\), the point is irregular (or singular).
Example 2: Cone Tangent Plane and Singularity

Find the tangent plane to the cone \(x^2 + y^2 = z^2\) at \((3, 4, 5)\) and \((0, 0, 0)\).

Using polar parametrization: \(x = r \cos \theta, y = r \sin \theta, z = r\):

\[ T_r = \langle \cos \theta, \sin \theta, 1 \rangle, \quad T_\theta = \langle -r \sin \theta, r \cos \theta, 0 \rangle \] \[ T_r \times T_\theta = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ \cos \theta & \sin \theta & 1 \\ -r \sin \theta & r \cos \theta & 0 \end{vmatrix} = \langle -r \cos \theta, -r \sin \theta, r \rangle \]

At \((3, 4, 5)\), \(r=5, \cos \theta = 3/5, \sin \theta = 4/5\). The normal is \(\langle -3, -4, 5 \rangle\). The plane is:

\[ -3(x-3) - 4(y-4) + 5(z-5) = 0 \]

At \((0, 0, 0)\), \(r=0 \Rightarrow T_r \times T_\theta = \langle 0, 0, 0 \rangle\). This is an irregular point; no unique tangent plane exists at the vertex of the cone.

Visual Description: A sketch of a double cone meeting at the origin. A smooth tangent plane is shown touching the side of the cone at (3,4,5), while the origin is highlighted as a sharp point where a plane cannot be uniquely defined.
Example 3: Checking for Irregular Points on a Graph

Find all irregular points on the graph of \(f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2 - 4x - 2y\).

Any graph \(z = f(x,y)\) can be parametrized as \(\vec{r}(x,y) = \langle x, y, f(x,y) \rangle\).

\[ T_x = \langle 1, 0, f_x \rangle = \langle 1, 0, 2x-4 \rangle \] \[ T_y = \langle 0, 1, f_y \rangle = \langle 0, 1, 2y-2 \rangle \] \[ T_x \times T_y = \langle -f_x, -f_y, 1 \rangle = \langle -(2x-4), -(2y-2), 1 \rangle \]

Because the \(\mathbf{k}\) component is constant at 1, the normal vector can never be \(\vec{0}\). Thus, there are no irregular points on this surface.

Surface Area of Parametrized Surfaces

Theorem: Surface Area Integral
The area of a surface \(S\) parametrized by \(\vec{r}(u, v)\) over a region \(D\) is: \[ \text{Area}(S) = \iint_D ||T_u \times T_v|| \, du \, dv \]
Example 4: Calculating Surface Area

Set up an integral to compute the area of the surface defined by \(\vec{r}(u,v) = \langle e^u \cos v, e^u \sin v, u \rangle\) for \(0 \le u \le 1\) and \(0 \le v \le \pi\).

Step 1: Find tangent vectors.

\[ T_u = \langle e^u \cos v, e^u \sin v, 1 \rangle, \quad T_v = \langle -e^u \sin v, e^u \cos v, 0 \rangle \]

Step 2: Compute the cross product.

\[ T_u \times T_v = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ e^u \cos v & e^u \sin v & 1 \\ -e^u \sin v & e^u \cos v & 0 \end{vmatrix} = \langle -e^u \cos v, -e^u \sin v, e^{2u} \rangle \]

Step 3: Calculate the magnitude.

\[ ||T_u \times T_v|| = \sqrt{(-e^u \cos v)^2 + (-e^u \sin v)^2 + (e^{2u})^2} \] \[ = \sqrt{e^{2u}(\cos^2 v + \sin^2 v) + e^{4u}} = \sqrt{e^{2u} + e^{4u}} = e^u \sqrt{1 + e^{2u}} \]

Step 4: Set up and evaluate the integral.

\[ \text{Area} = \int_0^1 \int_0^\pi e^u \sqrt{1 + e^{2u}} \, dv \, du = \pi \int_0^1 e^u \sqrt{1 + e^{2u}} . du \]
Example 2: Area of a Plane Bounded by a Cylinder

Compute the area on the plane \(x + y + z = 2\) bounded by the cylinder \(x^2 + y^2 \le 1\).

Step 1: Parametrize the surface.

To compute the area of the portion of the plane within the cylinder, we use polar coordinates for the region \(D\) defined by \(x^2 + y^2 \le 1\). The parametrization \(\vec{r}(r, \theta)\) is:

  • \(x = r \cos \theta\)
  • \(y = r \sin \theta\)
  • \(z = 2 - x - y = 2 - r \cos \theta - r \sin \theta\)

The parameter bounds are \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\) and \(0 \le r \le 1\).

Visual Description: A 3D coordinate system showing a vertical cylinder centered at the origin with radius 1. A slanted plane \(x + y + z = 2\) cuts through the cylinder, creating an elliptical surface area inside the cylinder's walls.

Step 2: Compute tangent vectors \(T_r\) and \(T_\theta\).

We take the partial derivatives of the parametrization \(\vec{r}(r, \theta) = \langle r \cos \theta, r \sin \theta, 2 - r \cos \theta - r \sin \theta \rangle\):

\[ T_r = \frac{\partial \vec{r}}{\partial r} = \langle \cos \theta, \sin \theta, -\cos \theta - \sin \theta \rangle \] \[ T_\theta = \frac{\partial \vec{r}}{\partial \theta} = \langle -r \sin \theta, r \cos \theta, r \sin \theta - r \cos \theta \rangle \]

Step 3: Compute the cross product \(T_r \times T_\theta\).

The normal vector is found using the determinant of the cross product matrix:

\[ T_r \times T_\theta = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ \cos \theta & \sin \theta & -(\cos \theta + \sin \theta) \\ -r \sin \theta & r \cos \theta & r(\sin \theta - \cos \theta) \end{vmatrix} \]

Calculating the components individually:

i-component: \(\sin \theta [r(\sin \theta - \cos \theta)] - [r \cos \theta (-(\cos \theta + \sin \theta))]\)

\[ = r \sin^2 \theta - r \sin \theta \cos \theta + r \cos^2 \theta + r \cos \theta \sin \theta = r(\sin^2 \theta + \cos^2 \theta) = r \]

j-component: \(-\left( \cos \theta [r(\sin \theta - \cos \theta)] - [-r \sin \theta (-(\cos \theta + \sin \theta))] \right)\)

\[ = -(r \cos \theta \sin \theta - r \cos^2 \theta - r \sin \theta \cos \theta - r \sin^2 \theta) = -(-r(\cos^2 \theta + \sin^2 \theta)) = r \]

k-component: \(\cos \theta (r \cos \theta) - (-r \sin \theta)(\sin \theta) = r \cos^2 \theta + r \sin^2 \theta = r \)

\[ T_r \times T_\theta = \langle r, r, r \rangle \]

Step 4: Compute the magnitude and evaluate the integral.

The magnitude of the normal vector represents the differential area element \(dS\):

\[ ||T_r \times T_\theta|| = \sqrt{r^2 + r^2 + r^2} = \sqrt{3r^2} = r\sqrt{3} \]

Now, integrate over the region \(D\):

\[ \text{Area}(S) = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{0}^{1} r\sqrt{3} \, dr \, d\theta \] \[ \text{Area}(S) = \sqrt{3} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \left[ \frac{1}{2}r^2 \right]_{0}^{1} \, d\theta \] \[ \text{Area}(S) = \sqrt{3} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \frac{1}{2} \, d\theta = \sqrt{3} \left( \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2\pi \right) = \pi\sqrt{3} \]

Scalar Surface Integrals

A surface integral of a scalar function \(f(x, y, z)\) over a surface \(S\) is the 3D version of a line integral. It is defined by:

\[ \iint_S f(x, y, z) \, dS = \iint_D f(\vec{r}(u,v)) ||T_u \times T_v|| \, du \, dv \]

If \(f(x, y, z) = 1\), the integral simply yields the surface area of \(S\). If \(f\) represents a mass density, the integral yields the total mass of the surface.
