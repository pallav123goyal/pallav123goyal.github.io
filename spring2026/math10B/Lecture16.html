Lecture 16: Green's Theorem

Recall: Let \(C\) be a simple closed curve in the plane with positive (counterclockwise) orientation.

Theorem (Green's Theorem): Given a vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = P(x,y)\hat{i} + Q(x,y)\hat{j}\) where \(P\) and \(Q\) have continuous partial derivatives on an open region containing \(D\), we have the equality:

\[\oint_{C^{+}} P \, dx + Q \, dy = \iint_{D} \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right) dA\]

where \(D\) is the plane region enclosed by the curve \(C\). This can also be written in shorthand notation as:

\[\iint_{D} (Q_x - P_y) \, dA\]

Worked Examples and Applications

Example 1: Evaluating a Line Integral Enclosing a Trapezoidal Region

Evaluate the line integral \(\oint_{C} 2xy \, dx + xy^2 \, dy\) along the closed curve \(C\).

Visual Description of the Curve \(C\): The region \(D\) is a four-sided polygonal domain (a trapezoid) in the first quadrant of the Cartesian plane. The boundary curve \(C\) consists of four directed line segments forming a counterclockwise loop:
  • A horizontal lower segment along the x-axis from the origin \((0,0)\) to \((2,0)\), parameterized by \(y = 0\).
  • A vertical right segment going upward from \((2,0)\) to \((2,3)\), parameterized by \(x = 2\).
  • A slanted upper boundary segment going down and left from \((2,3)\) to \((0,2)\), along the line \(y = 2 + \frac{x}{2}\).
  • A vertical left segment going downward along the y-axis from \((0,2)\) back to the origin \((0,0)\), parameterized by \(x = 0\).

Solution: If we were to compute this line integral directly, we would need to set up and evaluate a sum of four separate line integrals, one corresponding to each straight line segment of the boundary.

Instead, we can apply Green's Theorem. Identify the component functions of the vector field:

\[P(x,y) = 2xy \quad \text{and} \quad Q(x,y) = xy^2\]

Next, compute the required partial derivatives:

\[\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = y^2 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 2x\]

By Green's Theorem, the line integral is equivalent to the double integral over the enclosed region \(D\):

\[\iint_{D} (Q_x - P_y) \, dA = \iint_{D} (y^2 - 2x) \, dA\]

To evaluate the double integral, we establish the bounding inequalities for the region \(D\):

\[0 \le x \le 2\] \[0 \le y \le 2 + \frac{x}{2}\]

Setting up the iterated integral yields:

\[\int_{0}^{2} \int_{0}^{2+\frac{x}{2}} (y^2 - 2x) \, dy \, dx\]

First, integrate with respect to \(y\):

\[\int_{0}^{2} \left[ \frac{y^3}{3} - 2xy \right]_{y=0}^{y=2+\frac{x}{2}} dx = \int_{0}^{2} \left( \frac{1}{3}\left(2 + \frac{x}{2}\right)^3 - 2x\left(2 + \frac{x}{2}\right) \right) dx\]

Expanding the algebraic terms inside the integrand gives:

\[\int_{0}^{2} \frac{x^3 - 12x^2 - 48x + 64}{24} \, dx\]

Evaluating this single integral with respect to \(x\) over the limits from 0 to 2 results in the final value:

\[\text{Result} = \frac{1}{6}\]
Example 2: Verification of Green's Theorem on a Unit Circle

Verify Green's Theorem for the vector field components \(P(x,y) = x\) and \(Q(x,y) = xy\), where the curve \(C^+\) is the positively oriented unit circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\).

Step 1: Line Integral Evaluation

We parameterize the unit circle \(C^+\) using standard polar angle \(\theta\):

\[x = \cos\theta, \quad y = \sin\theta, \quad 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\]

The differentials are given by:

\[dx = -\sin\theta \, d\theta, \quad dy = \cos\theta \, d\theta\]

Substituting the parameterization into the component functions yields:

\[P(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) = \cos\theta\] \[Q(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) = \cos\theta\sin\theta\]

Setting up the line integral with respect to \(\theta\):

\[\oint_{C^+} P \, dx + Q \, dy = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \left( \cos\theta(-\sin\theta) + \cos\theta\sin\theta(\cos\theta) \right) d\theta\] \[= \int_{0}^{2\pi} \left( -\cos\theta\sin\theta + \cos^2\theta\sin\theta \right) d\theta\]

We evaluate this by using the substitution \(u = \cos\theta\), which gives \(du = -\sin\theta \, d\theta\):

\[= \left[ \frac{\cos^2\theta}{2} - \frac{\cos^3\theta}{3} \right]_{0}^{2\pi} = \left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3}\right) - \left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3}\right) = 0\]

Step 2: Double Surface Integral Evaluation

We evaluate the double integral \(\iint_{D} (Q_x - P_y) \, dA\) over the interior of the unit disk \(D\). Given \(Q(x,y) = xy\) and \(P(x,y) = x\), we find:

\[Q_x = y, \quad P_y = 0 \implies Q_x - P_y = y\]

Switching to polar coordinates where \(x = r\cos\theta\), \(y = r\sin\theta\), and \(dA = r \, dr \, d\theta\), the region \(D\) is described by \(0 \le r \le 1\) and \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\):

\[\iint_{D} y \, dA = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{0}^{1} (r\sin\theta) \, r \, dr \, d\theta = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{0}^{1} r^2\sin\theta \, dr \, d\theta\]

Integrating with respect to \(r\):

\[\int_{0}^{2\pi} \left[ \frac{r^3}{3} \right]_{0}^{1} \sin\theta \, d\theta = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \frac{1}{3}\sin\theta \, d\theta\]

Integrating with respect to \(\theta\):

\[= \left[ -\frac{1}{3}\cos\theta \right]_{0}^{2\pi} = -\frac{1}{3}(1 - 1) = 0\]

Since both the line integral and the double surface integral equal \(0\), Green's Theorem is verified.

Example 3: Line Integral Around a Centered Square Boundary

Consider a square region with vertices at \((-2,-2)\), \((-2,2)\), \((2,2)\), and \((2,-2)\). Evaluate the following integrals along its boundary curve \(C\):

a) \(\oint_{C} x \, dx + y \, dy\)

b) \(\oint_{C} y \, dx - x \, dy\)

Visual Description of the Square Boundary: The region is a square centered at the origin \((0,0)\) of the Cartesian plane. Its sides are aligned parallel to the coordinate axes, extending from \(-2\) to \(2\) along both the x and y dimensions. The four vertices are located at \((-2,2)\) in Quadrant II, \((2,2)\) in Quadrant I, \((2,-2)\) in Quadrant IV, and \((-2,-2)\) in Quadrant III.

Solution: Instead of performing direct calculations across the four independent vertical and horizontal paths, we transform the line integrals into double surface integrals over the interior square region via Green's Theorem.

Part a) Calculation:

Here, \(P = x\) and \(Q = y\). Evaluating the integrand for the double integral yields:

\[Q_x = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(y) = 0, \quad P_y = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x) = 0 \implies Q_x - P_y = 0\] \[\oint_{C} x \, dx + y \, dy = \iint_{D} 0 \, dA = 0\]

Part b) Calculation:

Here, \(P = y\) and \(Q = -x\). Computing the partial derivatives gives:

\[Q_x = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(-x) = -1, \quad P_y = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(y) = 1 \implies Q_x - P_y = -1 - 1 = -2\]

Applying Green's Theorem:

\[\oint_{C} y \, dx - x \, dy = \iint_{D} -2 \, dA = -2 \iint_{D} dA\]

The remaining expression is simply \(-2\) multiplied by the total area of the square. Since each side of the square has a length of \(4\), the area is \(4 \times 4 = 16\):

\[= -2 \times (\text{Area of Square}) = -2(16) = -32\]

Area Formulas Derived from Green's Theorem

Following the structural logic of the previous example, we can formulate a general method to calculate the plane area of any enclosed region \(D\) bounded by a simple closed curve \(C\).

If we set components such that \(Q_x - P_y = 1\), the double integral simplifies directly to the surface area of \(D\). For instance, choosing \(P = -y\) and \(Q = 0\) produces \(Q_x - P_y = 1\). Similarly, choosing \(P = 0\) and \(Q = x\) gives \(Q_x - P_y = 1\). If we select a symmetric combination where \(P = -\frac{1}{2}y\) and \(Q = \frac{1}{2}x\), then:

\[Q_x - P_y = \frac{1}{2} - \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = 1\]

This yields the standard area formulas via line integrals:

\[\text{Area}(D) = \oint_{C} x \, dy = -\oint_{C} y \, dx = \frac{1}{2} \oint_{C} x \, dy - y \, dx\]
Example 4: Area Enclosed by a Parametric Curve

Compute the area enclosed inside the parametric curve \(C(t)\) defined by:

\[\mathbf{c}(t) = \sin(2t)\hat{i} + \sin(t)\hat{j} \quad \text{for } 0 \le t \le \pi\]

Solution: We apply the symmetric boundary coordinate area formula:

\[\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \oint_{C} x \, dy - y \, dx\]

From the parametric vector equation, the coordinates and their corresponding differentials are:

\[x = \sin(2t) \implies dx = 2\cos(2t) \, dt\] \[y = \sin(t) \implies dy = \cos(t) \, dt\]

Recall the trigonometric double-angle identity: \(\sin(2t) = 2\sin(t)\cos(t)\). Substituting these parametric forms into our line integral expressions produces:

\[\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{\pi} \left( \sin(2t)\cos(t) - \sin(t) \cdot 2\cos(2t) \right) dt\]

Expanding using identities transforms the integral to:

\[= \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{\pi} \left( 2\sin(t)\cos^2(t) - 2\sin(t)(\cos^2(t) - \sin^2(t)) \right) dt\] \[= \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{\pi} 2\sin^3(t) \, dt = \int_{0}^{\pi} \sin^3(t) \, dt\]

To integrate \(\sin^3(t)\), we split off one \(\sin(t)\) factor and use the identity \(\sin^2(t) = 1 - \cos^2(t)\):

\[= \int_{0}^{\pi} \sin^2(t)\sin(t) \, dt = \int_{0}^{\pi} (1 - \cos^2(t))\sin(t) \, dt\]

Using substitution with \(u = \cos(t)\) and \(du = -\sin(t) \, dt\), the integration evaluates to:

\[= \left[ -\cos(t) + \frac{\cos^3(t)}{3} \right]_{0}^{\pi} = \left( 1 - \frac{1}{3} \right) - \left( -1 + \frac{1}{3} \right) = \frac{2}{3} + \frac{2}{3} = \frac{4}{3}\]

Thus, the enclosed plane area is \(\frac{4}{3}\).

Generalizations of Green's Theorem

Green's Theorem bridges a line integral around a closed curve to a double integral over a flat two-dimensional region. This concept serves as a multi-variable version of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, which states that the integral of a derivative over an interval is determined by the values of the function at its boundaries:

\[\int_{a}^{b} F'(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(a)\]

When extending these multi-variable relationships into higher three-dimensional dimensional space (\(\mathbb{R}^3\)), we utilize generalized differential operations on vector fields \(\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q, R \rangle\):

  • Divergence: Related to the vector dot product, denoted as \(\text{div }\mathbf{F} = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial z}\).
  • Curl: Related to the vector cross product, denoted as \(\text{curl }\mathbf{F} = \nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \left\langle \frac{\partial R}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z}, \frac{\partial P}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial R}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right\rangle\).

These operations form the mathematical foundation for Stokes' Theorem and the Divergence Theorem, which generalize boundary relationships across higher dimensions.
