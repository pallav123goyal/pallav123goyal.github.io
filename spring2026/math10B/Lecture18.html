Lecture 18: Conservative Vector Fields and Gauss' theorem

Example 1

Suppose \(\mathbf{F} = y\hat{\mathbf{i}} - x\hat{\mathbf{j}} + e^{xz}\hat{\mathbf{k}}\). Compute the surface integral of \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F}\) on the upper hemisphere of the unit sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\).

Solution:

First, let's look at what happens if we attempt to compute this surface integral directly. We compute the curl of the vector field \(\mathbf{F}\):

\[\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{i}} & \hat{\mathbf{j}} & \hat{\mathbf{k}} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ y & -x & e^{xz} \end{vmatrix} = \left( 0 - 0 \right)\hat{\mathbf{i}} - \left( ze^{xz} - 0 \right)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + \left( -1 - 1 \right)\hat{\mathbf{k}} = -ze^{xz}\hat{\mathbf{j}} - 2\hat{\mathbf{k}}\]

To compute the integral directly, we would need to parametrize the upper hemispherical surface \(S\):

\[x = \sin\phi \cos\theta\] \[y = \sin\phi \sin\theta\] \[z = \cos\phi\]

where the parameter bounds are defined by \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\) and \(0 \le \phi \le \pi/2\). Computing the tangent vectors yields:

\[\mathbf{T}_\theta = \langle -\sin\phi \sin\theta, \sin\phi \cos\theta, 0 \rangle\] \[\mathbf{T}_\phi = \langle \cos\phi \cos\theta, \cos\phi \sin\theta, -\sin\phi \rangle\]

The normal vector is obtained by their cross product \(\mathbf{T}_\phi \times \mathbf{T}_\theta\). Substituting these values into the flux integral \(\iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}\) results in a complicated integration process due to the non-linear transcendental term \(-ze^{xz}\).

Instead, let's use Stokes' theorem to determine if evaluating the corresponding line integral over the boundary curve is simpler:

\[\iint_{S} (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \oint_{\partial S} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\]

The surface \(S\) is the upper hemisphere of the sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\).

Visual Description of the Domain: A 3D Cartesian coordinate system showing an upper hemisphere centered at the origin. The circular boundary curve \(\partial S\) lies perfectly flat on the \(xy\)-plane where \(z=0\), forming a circle of radius 1 centered at the origin.

The boundary curve \(\partial S\) is the unit circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\) lying entirely within the \(xy\)-plane. We can parametrize this closed boundary path \(\partial S\) directly with respect to \(\theta\):

\[x = \cos\theta\] \[y = \sin\theta\] \[z = 0\]

where \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\). This gives the vector-valued position function:

\[\mathbf{r}(\theta) = \langle \cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0 \rangle\]

Taking the derivative yields the velocity vector:

\[\mathbf{r}'(\theta) = \langle -\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0 \rangle\]

Next, we evaluate the vector field \(\mathbf{F} = y\hat{\mathbf{i}} - x\hat{\mathbf{j}} + e^{xz}\hat{\mathbf{k}}\) restricted to our boundary path where \(z=0\):

\[\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(\theta)) = \sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{i}} - \cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{j}} + e^{(\cos\theta)(0)}\hat{\mathbf{k}} = \sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{i}} - \cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 1\hat{\mathbf{k}} = \langle \sin\theta, -\cos\theta, 1 \rangle\]

Now, we evaluate the line integral by computing the dot product \(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(\theta)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(\theta)\):

\[\oint_{\partial S} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \langle \sin\theta, -\cos\theta, 1 \rangle \cdot \langle -\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0 \rangle \, d\theta\] \[= \int_{0}^{2\pi} \left( -\sin^2\theta - \cos^2\theta + 0 \right) d\theta\] \[= \int_{0}^{2\pi} -1 \, d\theta = -2\pi\]

Conservative Vector Fields

Definition: A vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) is said to be conservative on a region if its curl is equal to zero everywhere within that domain:

\[\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\]

An equivalent condition is that the line integral along any closed curve \(\gamma\) within the region vanishes identically:

\[\oint_{\gamma} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0\]

Consider any two distinct points \(P\) and \(Q\) in space connected by two separate paths, \(\gamma_1\) and \(\gamma_2\). Because the combination of moving forward along \(\gamma_1\) and returning along the reverse direction of \(\gamma_2\) constitutes a closed loop path, the total path integral around this loop is zero:

\[\int_{\gamma_1} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} - \int_{\gamma_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0 \implies \int_{\gamma_1} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_{\gamma_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\]

This demonstrates that the line integral of a conservative vector field from point \(P\) to point \(Q\) is completely independent of the path chosen between them.

Consequently, there must exist a scalar potential function \(G\) acting as an antiderivative for the field, such that its gradient fields match \(\mathbf{F}\):

\[\text{grad}(G) = \nabla G = \mathbf{F}\]
Example 2

Given the vector field \(\mathbf{F} = \langle 2x, \sin z, y\cos z \rangle\):

  • a) Verify that \(\mathbf{F}\) is a conservative vector field.
  • b) Find a scalar potential function \(G\) such that \(\nabla G = \mathbf{F}\).

Solution for part a):

We compute the curl vector \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F}\):

\[\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{i}} & \hat{\mathbf{j}} & \hat{\mathbf{k}} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ 2x & \sin z & y\cos z \end{vmatrix}\] \[= \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(y\cos z) - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(\sin z) \right)\hat{\mathbf{i}} - \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(y\cos z) - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(2x) \right)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\sin z) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2x) \right)\hat{\mathbf{k}}\] \[= (\cos z - \cos z)\hat{\mathbf{i}} - (0 - 0)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + (0 - 0)\hat{\mathbf{k}} = \mathbf{0}\]

Since \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\), the vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) is verified to be conservative.

Solution for part b):

We must find a function \(G(x,y,z)\) whose partial derivatives satisfy \(\nabla G = \langle \frac{\partial G}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial G}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial G}{\partial z} \rangle = \mathbf{F}\). This sets up three simultaneous equations:

  1. \(\frac{\partial G}{\partial x} = 2x\)
  2. \(\frac{\partial G}{\partial y} = \sin z\)
  3. \(\frac{\partial G}{\partial z} = y\cos z\)

Integrating our first relation with respect to \(x\):

\[\frac{\partial G}{\partial x} = 2x \implies G(x,y,z) = x^2 + g_1(y,z)\]

Integrating our second relation with respect to \(y\):

\[\frac{\partial G}{\partial y} = \sin z \implies G(x,y,z) = y\sin z + g_2(x,z)\]

Integrating our third relation with respect to \(z\):

\[\frac{\partial G}{\partial z} = y\cos z \implies G(x,y,z) = y\sin z + g_3(x,y)\]

Reconciling all three expressions for \(G(x,y,z)\), we conclude that the general scalar potential function is:

\[G(x,y,z) = x^2 + y\sin z + C\]
Example 3

Consider the vector field \(\mathbf{F} = \langle 2xz, 2z, x^2 + 2y \rangle\). Integrate \(\mathbf{F}\) along a path from the point \((0,0,0)\) to the point \((2,1,5)\) along two distinct trajectories:

  • The straight line segment connecting the two points.
  • A semicircular arc connecting them within the plane defined by \(x - 2y = 0\).

Solution:

Let's check if the field is conservative first by computing \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F}\):

\[\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{i}} & \hat{\mathbf{j}} & \hat{\mathbf{k}} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ 2xz & 2z & x^2 + 2y \end{vmatrix}\] \[= \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^2+2y) - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(2z)\right)\hat{\mathbf{i}} - \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2+2y) - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(2xz)\right)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(2z) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2xz)\right)\hat{\mathbf{k}}\] \[= (2 - 2)\hat{\mathbf{i}} - (2x - 2x)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + (0 - 0)\hat{\mathbf{k}} = \mathbf{0}\]

Since \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\), \(\mathbf{F}\) is a conservative field and we can find a scalar potential function \(G\) such that \(\nabla G = \mathbf{F}\).

We find \(G\) by setting up the individual system of component matches:

  1. \(\frac{\partial G}{\partial x} = 2xz \implies G(x,y,z) = x^2z + g_1(y,z)\)
  2. \(\frac{\partial G}{\partial y} = 2z \implies G(x,y,z) = 2yz + g_2(x,z)\)
  3. \(\frac{\partial G}{\partial z} = x^2 + 2y \implies G(x,y,z) = x^2z + 2yz + g_3(x,y)\)

Combining these partial integration constraints yields the common potential function solution:

\[G(x,y,z) = x^2z + 2yz\]

Because \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative, the path independence property ensures that the line integral value is identical regardless of whether we follow the linear path or the semicircular arc. Furthermore, we can apply the Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals:

\[\int_{(0,0,0)}^{(2,1,5)} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_{(0,0,0)}^{(2,1,5)} \nabla G \cdot d\mathbf{r} = G(2,1,5) - G(0,0,0)\]

Plugging the coordinates into our potential function formula:

\[G(2,1,5) = (2)^2(5) + 2(1)(5) = 20 + 10 = 30\] \[G(0,0,0) = (0)^2(0) + 2(0)(0) = 0\] \[\int_{(0,0,0)}^{(2,1,5)} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 30 - 0 = 30\]

Summary of Integral Theorems

The core framework of multi-variable vector operations connects line integrals, surface integrals, and volume integrals through fundamental equivalence principles.

Stokes' Theorem: Given a vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) and an orientable surface \(S\) bounded by a closed piecewise-smooth boundary curve \(\partial S\):

\[\iint_{S} (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \oint_{\partial S} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\]

Gauss' Theorem (Divergence Theorem): Given a vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) and a bounded solid region \(R\) in space enclosed by a smooth boundary surface \(\partial R\):

\[\iiint_{R} (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}) \, dV = \iint_{\partial R} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}\]
