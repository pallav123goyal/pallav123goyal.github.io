Question 1 (20 points)

Compute the double integral:

\[ \int_0^1 \int_{\sqrt{y}}^1 e^{x^3} \, dx \, dy \]

Solution:

The inner integral with respect to \( x \) cannot be evaluated using elementary functions. Therefore, we must change the order of integration.

Step 1: Identify the region of integration

The current bounds are \( \sqrt{y} \le x \le 1 \) and \( 0 \le y \le 1 \). This describes a region bounded on the left by the parabola \( x = \sqrt{y} \) (which is \( y = x^2 \)) and on the right by the line \( x = 1 \).

Step 2: Change the order to \( dy \, dx \)

Looking at the region vertically, for a fixed \( x \), \( y \) ranges from the \( x \)-axis (\( y = 0 \)) up to the parabola (\( y = x^2 \)). The total range for \( x \) is from \( 0 \) to \( 1 \).

\[ \int_0^1 \int_0^{x^2} e^{x^3} \, dy \, dx \]

Step 3: Evaluate the integral

Evaluate the inner integral with respect to \( y \):

\[ \int_0^{x^2} e^{x^3} \, dy = [y \cdot e^{x^3}]_0^{x^2} = x^2 e^{x^3} \]

Now, evaluate the outer integral with respect to \( x \) using \( u \)-substitution. Let \( u = x^3 \), then \( du = 3x^2 \, dx \), or \( x^2 \, dx = \frac{1}{3}du \):

\[ \int_0^1 x^2 e^{x^3} \, dx = \frac{1}{3} \int_0^1 e^u \, du \] \[ = \frac{1}{3} [e^u]_0^1 = \frac{1}{3}(e^1 - e^0) = \frac{e - 1}{3} \]

Final Answer: \( \frac{e - 1}{3} \)

Question 2 (18 + 7 points)

Set up a triple integral to compute the volume enclosed by the surfaces \( y+x^2=1 \), \( z=1 \), \( y=0 \) and \( z=1-y \). Evaluate the integral.

Solution:

1. Setting up the bounds:

In the \(xy\)-plane, the region is bounded by \( y = 1 - x^2 \) and \( y = 0 \). Setting them equal gives \( x = \pm 1 \). So, \( -1 \le x \le 1 \) and \( 0 \le y \le 1 - x^2 \).

For \( z \), the volume is bounded below by \( z = 1 - y \) and above by \( z = 1 \).

2. Setting up the integral:

\[ V = \int_{-1}^1 \int_0^{1-x^2} \int_{1-y}^1 1 \, dz \, dy \, dx \]

3. Evaluating the integral:

Inner integral (\( z \)):

\[ \int_{1-y}^1 dz = [z]_{1-y}^1 = 1 - (1 - y) = y \]

Middle integral (\( y \)):

\[ \int_0^{1-x^2} y \, dy = \left[ \frac{1}{2}y^2 \right]_0^{1-x^2} = \frac{1}{2}(1 - x^2)^2 = \frac{1}{2}(1 - 2x^2 + x^4) \]

Outer integral (\( x \)):

\[ \int_{-1}^1 \frac{1}{2}(1 - 2x^2 + x^4) \, dx = \frac{1}{2} \left[ x - \frac{2}{3}x^3 + \frac{1}{5}x^5 \right]_{-1}^1 \] \[ = \frac{1}{2} \left( \left(1 - \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{5}\right) - \left(-1 + \frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{5}\right) \right) = 1 - \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{5} \] \[ = \frac{15}{15} - \frac{10}{15} + \frac{3}{15} = \frac{8}{15} \]

Final Answer: \( \frac{8}{15} \)

Question 3 (BONUS) (5 points)

Set up a triple integral for the problem on the previous page swapping the order of integration of the first two variables. (That is, if you set up the integral as \( dx \, dy \, dz \) on the previous page, set it up as \( dy \, dx \, dz \) here.) It is okay if your answer is a sum of two triple integrals.

Solution:

To express the integral in the order \( dy \, dz \, dx \), we look at the projections of the solid onto the coordinate axes and planes, starting with the outermost variable \( x \).

1. x-bounds (Outer):

As determined in Question 2, the solid exists where the parabolic cylinder \( y = 1 - x^2 \) intersects the plane \( y = 0 \). This occurs between \( x = -1 \) and \( x = 1 \).

2. z-bounds (Middle):

For a fixed \( x \), we need to find the range of \( z \). The surfaces bounding \( z \) are the planes \( z = 1 - y \) and \( z = 1 \). Since \( y \) ranges from \( 0 \) to \( 1 - x^2 \):

  • When \( y = 0 \), \( z = 1 - 0 = 1 \).
  • When \( y = 1 - x^2 \), \( z = 1 - (1 - x^2) = x^2 \).

Thus, for a fixed \( x \), \( z \) ranges from \( x^2 \) to \( 1 \).

3. y-bounds (Inner):

For fixed \( x \) and \( z \), we determine the bounds for \( y \). The lower bound is given by the plane \( z = 1 - y \), which we rewrite as \( y = 1 - z \). The upper bound is the parabolic cylinder \( y = 1 - x^2 \).

The integral in the order \( dy \, dz \, dx \):

\[ V = \int_{-1}^1 \int_{x^2}^1 \int_{1-z}^{1-x^2} 1 \, dy \, dz \, dx \]
Question 4 (25 points)

Evaluate the following integral over the region \( R \) enclosed between the spherical shells \( x^2+y^2+z^2=4 \) and \( x^2+y^2+z^2=9 \) above the \( xy \)-plane:

\[ \iiint_R \frac{3}{1+(\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2})^3} dV \]

Solution:

We use spherical coordinates: \( x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = \rho^2 \) and \( dV = \rho^2 \sin\phi \, d\rho \, d\phi \, d\theta \).

Bounds:

  • Between spheres of radius 2 and 3: \( 2 \le \rho \le 3 \)
  • Above the xy-plane (\( z \ge 0 \)): \( 0 \le \phi \le \frac{\pi}{2} \)
  • Full rotation around z-axis: \( 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi \)

The integral becomes:

\[ \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\pi/2} \int_2^3 \frac{3}{1+\rho^3} \rho^2 \sin\phi \, d\rho \, d\phi \, d\theta \]

Because the limits are constants, we can separate the integrals:

\[ = \left( \int_0^{2\pi} d\theta \right) \left( \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin\phi \, d\phi \right) \left( \int_2^3 \frac{3\rho^2}{1+\rho^3} \, d\rho \right) \]

1. \( \int_0^{2\pi} d\theta = 2\pi \)

2. \( \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin\phi \, d\phi = [-\cos\phi]_0^{\pi/2} = 0 - (-1) = 1 \)

3. Let \( u = 1 + \rho^3 \), then \( du = 3\rho^2 d\rho \). For \( \rho=2, u=9 \). For \( \rho=3, u=28 \).

\[ \int_9^{28} \frac{1}{u} \, du = [\ln|u|]_9^{28} = \ln(28) - \ln(9) = \ln\left(\frac{28}{9}\right) \]

Multiplying these together:

\[ = 2\pi \cdot 1 \cdot \ln\left(\frac{28}{9}\right) = 2\pi \ln\left(\frac{28}{9}\right) \]

Final Answer: \( 2\pi \ln\left(\frac{28}{9}\right) \)

Question 5 (30 points)

Compute the area enclosed by the curves \( xy=1 \), \( xy=2 \), \( xy^2=1 \) and \( xy^2=2 \) in the first quadrant of the \( xy \)-plane.

Solution:

We use the change of variables:

  • \( u = xy \)
  • \( v = xy^2 \)

The region \( S \) in the \( uv \)-plane is defined by \( 1 \le u \le 2 \) and \( 1 \le v \le 2 \).

Step 1: Express \( x \) and \( y \) in terms of \( u \) and \( v \).

Starting with the ratio of the two equations:

\[ \frac{v}{u} = \frac{xy^2}{xy} = y \implies y = \frac{v}{u} \]

Substitute \( y = \frac{v}{u} \) back into the equation for \( u \):

\[ u = x\left(\frac{v}{u}\right) \implies x = \frac{u^2}{v} \]

Step 2: Compute the Jacobian directly.

The Jacobian is given by \( J = \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} \):

\[ J = \begin{vmatrix} \frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} \\ \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} \frac{2u}{v} & -\frac{u^2}{v^2} \\ -\frac{v}{u^2} & \frac{1}{u} \end{vmatrix} \] \[ J = \left(\frac{2u}{v}\right)\left(\frac{1}{u}\right) - \left(-\frac{u^2}{v^2}\right)\left(-\frac{v}{u^2}\right) \] \[ J = \frac{2}{v} - \frac{v}{v^2} = \frac{2}{v} - \frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{v} \]

Step 3: Evaluate the area integral.

\[ A = \iint_R 1 \, dA = \int_1^2 \int_1^2 |J| \, du \, dv = \int_1^2 \int_1^2 \frac{1}{v} \, du \, dv \] \[ A = \left( \int_1^2 1 \, du \right) \left( \int_1^2 \frac{1}{v} \, dv \right) \] \[ A = [u]_1^2 \cdot [\ln|v|]_1^2 = (2-1) \cdot (\ln 2 - \ln 1) = \ln 2 \]

Final Answer: \( \ln 2 \)
